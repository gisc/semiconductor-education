Chapter 2 · The magic sand

Chip-making starts with quartz - the same mineral as beach sand - purified until only one atom in a billion is out of place. The result is silicon, a semiconductor: it conducts electricity far better than glass, far worse than copper. That awkward in-betweenness is exactly what makes it useful, because we can tune it.

The tuning trick is called doping: mixing in a pinch of another element. Try it yourself. Pick a dopant, then tap atoms in the crystal to replace them.

Pure silicon - a well-behaved crystal that barely conducts.

Chapter 3 · The tiny switch

In 1947, at Bell Labs in New Jersey, three scientists built the first transistor: a switch with no moving parts, opened and closed purely by electricity. It won them the Nobel Prize and quietly rewired the future. Every phone, satellite and AI since is, at bottom, a colossal crowd of these switches.

A transistor is a tap for electricity - and electricity itself is the hand that turns it.

One switch is a bit. Wire switches together and you get logic gates - tiny decision-makers. AND, OR, XOR, NAND: from these, engineers build adders, memory and entire processors. Everything any computer has ever done reduces to these few moves. Play with them:

Chapter 4 · Printing the invisible

A leading-edge chip holds tens of billions of transistors, each far smaller than a virus. No human hand could build one. Instead, chips are printed, in factories called fabs, using the most precise manufacturing process humanity has ever invented. Walk through it:

The price of precision

A single leading-edge fab can cost over US$20 billion to build, and the EUV printers inside it cost hundreds of millions each. Only a handful of companies on Earth can afford to play at the frontier.

The shrinking act

Moore's law

In 1965, Gordon Moore noticed that the number of transistors engineers could squeeze onto a chip was doubling roughly every two years. The trend held for half a century and remade the world. Drag the slider through time:

2,300 transistors

In 1971, 2,300 transistors filled a chip. Today one AI chip holds about 90 million times more - yet each transistor is far cheaper and smaller. That is Moore's law in action.

Chapter 5 · Why AI is hungry for chips

When an AI writes you a poem, what is it actually doing? Mostly one boring thing, repeated beyond imagination: multiply, add, multiply, add - trillions of times, in huge grids of numbers called matrices.

A modern CPU has a handful of very powerful cores, each racing through its work and clever enough to juggle several tasks at once. A GPU takes the opposite bet: thousands of simpler cores doing the same small job side by side. Neither is simply faster - they win at different work. Jobs that are the same tiny operation repeated massively, like AI matrix maths, suit the crowd of simple cores; work full of different steps and decisions suits the powerful few. Watch the pattern in a race - using made-up numbers chosen to show the pattern, not to benchmark real chips: real CPUs have a small number of cores, real GPUs have thousands, and every chip and workload is different.

This hunger reshaped the industry: AI chips now also need ultra-fast memory stacked beside them (HBM) and advanced packaging that fuses several chips into one. The race for better AI is, physically, a race for better chips - and for the fabs that make them.

Chapter 6 · The chip chessboard

No country can build a modern chip alone. Design, tools, manufacturing and packaging are scattered across the planet - which makes chips a matter of economics, security and geopolitics, not just engineering. Follow one chip's passport:

A supply-chain study by SEMI and DHL counted more than 1,000 processing steps and over 70 border crossings behind a single modern chip. What happens if just one stop closes?

Why governments care

During the 2020-2022 chip shortage, car factories around the world stood still for want of chips costing a few dollars. Since then, the US, Europe, Japan, India and others have poured tens of billions into building fabs at home. Chips are now treated like oil once was: a resource worth planning a nation around.

The challenger: Japan's big bet

Japan once made over half the world's chips, then fell behind. In 2022 its government and biggest companies launched Rapidus - a national comeback attempt backed by roughly US$15 billion of public money, aiming to mass-produce advanced 2-nanometre chips in Hokkaido from 2027. As of late 2026 the technology is on track, but the hardest question remains open: who will actually buy the chips? Catching up is not only an engineering problem.

Keep arguing about it

  1. If one company makes most of the world's most advanced chips, is that a triumph of specialisation or a danger to everyone? What would you do if you were a small country like Singapore?
  2. Moore's law is slowing as transistors approach the size of atoms. Should we keep shrinking - or rethink how computers are designed altogether?
  3. An AI data centre can use as much electricity as a small town. Who should get to decide how the world's chip-making and energy capacity is used?
  4. Chips designed in one country, printed with machines from another, made in a third, used everywhere. Who, if anyone, "owns" a chip?
  5. Japan is spending about US$15 billion to catch TSMC at 2 nanometres. If you ran Rapidus, how would you persuade customers to trust a newcomer?
